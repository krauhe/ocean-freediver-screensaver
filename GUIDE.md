# Ocean Freediver Screensaver · The Blue Deep

## Wreck exploration and glow controls

**Comb jellies** shows three enlarged, Mnemiopsis leidyi-inspired animals, independent of the general population slider. Eight segmented comb rows carry moving rainbow highlights in daylight or inside a scuba torch beam. A deliberate artificial viewing fill also makes the enlarged foreground specimens visible at night; this is a readability aid, not naturally emitted rainbow light. This represents light scattered by beating cilia, not rainbow bioluminescence. A nearby moving large animal can trigger a separate short green emission along the rows at night, governed by **Bioluminescence**; a stationary neighbour does not repeatedly trigger it. **Pause** freezes drift, comb animation and flash decay. These specimens are deliberately enlarged for the wide scene: the real animal is roughly 7–12 cm long. Geometry, optical spectra and propagation timing are visual approximations. Sources: [IUCN Mnemiopsis leidyi](https://iucn-medmis.org/en/species/mnemiopsis-leidyi), [Smithsonian on jellyfish and comb jellies](https://ocean.si.edu/ocean-life/invertebrates/jellyfish-and-comb-jellies).

Rising freediver and scuba bubbles also activate sparse nighttime plankton flashes in the narrow water wake just behind their edges. Larger bubbles disturb a little more water. Bubble wakes now have stronger, more frequent points slightly below the silver rims so the air bubbles do not hide the cyan flashes. The points remain in the water and fade, rather than sticking to the bubble or turning its surface into a lamp. This effect uses the existing **Bioluminescence** slider, switches off in daylight and freezes with **Pause**.

**Bottom life** toggles small goby-inspired bottom fish, shrimp, sea stars, rubble shelters and sediment. Bottom fish alternate resting, short foraging swims and retreat to shelter when a large animal or diver approaches. Shrimp favour shelter in daylight and make short backwards escape swims. Sea stars creep slowly without a fish-like flight response. The king crab alternates walking and foraging pauses and freezes when a large animal is close. These animals are also hidden with **Wreck & reef**.

Fish schools and sharks now have individual persistent position, velocity, goals and local sensing rather than prescribed elliptical paths. School fish separate, align, regroup and accelerate away from nearby large swimmers. Great whites keep wider spacing and do not align as a school; hammerheads have only a weak local alignment tendency. Sharks anticipate the reef, wreck, truck, dive line and nearby divers, and turn with limited acceleration. Their fins and tail effort follow actual swimming, and they no longer periodically gape without a feeding event. Jellyfish primarily drift with the shared current and their own bell pulses. Dragging and release inertia are preserved.

One mild current model drives fish advection, jellyfish drift, plant bending and suspended-particle drift, with reduced flow in the wreck's lee. Sediment is a small, non-luminous particle effect emitted by crab footfalls, moving bottom fish and moving diver fin tips close to the sand. It settles and fades; stationary or distant fins do not raise a cloud. **Pause** freezes autonomous behavior and sediment. The state machines, shelter mask and sediment are bounded visual approximations, not a calibrated ecosystem, fluid solver or species-complete behavior model.

**King crab** shows three spiny, articulated red king crabs walking independently across the sand in front of the ferry. Six legs alternate planted steps and lifted return strokes; the claws move independently. It follows Pause and Speed, and is also hidden when **Wreck & reef** is off. The reef shelf now carries additional rooted grass patches, with a compact small-fish school following its edge.

At the surface, slightly sharper crests develop sparse foam patches. Individual nighttime plankton flashes occur inside the crest-disturbance mask and follow the **Bioluminescence** slider. This is a lightweight visual approximation of breaking crests, not overturning fluid geometry or a physical foam simulation.

**Hemmoor truck** toggles the Kreidesee-inspired truck lying on its side in the sand behind the ferry. Its cab front faces the viewer and carries the KREIDESEE lettering; the tipper sides are unlettered.

**Wreck & reef** shows a Gozo-inspired upright ferry on sand, with open cabin windows, railings, companion stairs and a submerged limestone shelf on the right. **Scuba buddies** shows two divers circling the wreck slowly together at a steady depth. They use short frog kicks separated by glides, with many small regulator bubbles visible from the start and independent breathing cycles thereafter. At night their torches sweep across the boat; select **Custom time** and **00:00** to see the beams. Both controls are independent of the animal population slider. The scenery is an artistic model rather than a real-depth map of Karwela.

**Bioluminescence** sets the amount from 0–1200%. Start at 300%, select **Custom time**, then move the clock into the night to compare levels. Zero disables the disturbance glow; the lowest active setting is 100%, with 100% increments up to 1200%. The value is remembered locally where browser storage is available. Individual small points flash in the water swept by a moving blade, hand or body-separation edge. A stationary source emits nothing. Activated points remain behind in the water and fade; they are not attached to an animal. Activation uses a bounded swept-surface approximation rather than a full fluid simulation. The existing **Particles** slider controls suspended marine snow separately. **Pause** freezes swimming, bubbles and glow evolution.

Open **index.html** in Chrome or Edge. No installation, internet connection or server is required. Keep `index.html`, `ocean.css`, `ocean.js`, `jellyfish.js`, `diver-bubbles.js`, `freediver-rig.js`, `freediver.js`, `scuba.js`, `seabed.js`, `hemmoor-truck.js`, `grafisk/` and `three.min.js` together.

1. **Fullscreen:** Click Fullscreen, press F or double-click the open water. Press Esc to exit fullscreen.
2. **Pause:** Press Space or click Pause. Repeat to resume the animation.
3. **Speed:** Use the slider between the buttons.
4. **Animal count:** The Animals slider selects a total of 0–40 animals, divided as evenly as possible between the checked species, including jellyfish. With only one species checked, it controls that species alone. Some animals may swim outside the field of view. The scene starts with 30 animals. Small background fish school separately as autonomous scenery.
5. **Hide controls:** Click or tap the open water to minimize the controls, then select **Menu** to reopen them. Moving the pointer keeps the menu minimized. After 5 seconds of inactivity, the visible controls or Menu button fade out; moving the pointer or touching the screen reveals them again.
6. **Push, pull and turn:** Grab a shark or a jellyfish's bell. Hold the left mouse button to push it deeper into the water, or the right button to pull it closer. Move the mouse to steer sideways at the same time. A grip near the centre mainly moves the animal; a grip farther from the centre also rotates it. Animals follow elastically and retain some movement after release, then gradually recover their swimming attitude. Jellyfish tentacles yield to movement. One finger drags in the screen plane and can also turn the animal. These interactions work while paused, with the rest of the ocean remaining paused.
7. **Lighting time:** Drag the Lighting slider to any minute from 00:00 to 23:59. The time appears beside it in 24-hour HH:mm format, regardless of browser language. Moving the slider switches to Custom time and holds that lighting setting. With the slider focused, arrow keys adjust one minute; Home and End select 00:00 and 23:59. Choose Local clock to follow your computer again. Switching back to Custom time restores the last manual setting. You can adjust lighting while the animation is paused.

8. **Species:** Check Hammerhead, Great white and Jellyfish independently to combine them freely. The Animals slider redistributes its total between the checked species. Uncheck all three to leave just the water, small fish and particles; the slider retains its setting. Toggle the separate **Freedivers** checkbox to show or hide the deep training rig with its monofin athlete and safety diver independently of the animal population slider.
9. **Particles:** Select 0–5,000 suspended particles in steps of 100. The default is 1,700; zero hides them. Animal and particle counts can both be changed while paused.
10. **Graphics:** Choose between four performance modes:
    - **Eco · 24 fps:** 24 fps frame-rate cap, 1.0× pixel ratio, 512×512 caustic light map updated every second frame, 72 sunbeams, 60 background fish, and simplified distant shark geometry from 34 m for low power use.
    - **Balanced · 30 fps (default):** 30 fps cap, up to 1.35× pixel ratio, 512×512 caustic map updated at 30 Hz, 108 sunbeams, 95 background fish, and distant shark LOD beyond 48 m.
    - **High · 40 fps:** 40 fps cap, up to 1.6× pixel ratio, denser 512×512 caustic map updated at 40 Hz, 132 sunbeams, 110 background fish, and distant shark LOD beyond 64 m.
    - **Ultra · 60 fps:** Smooth 60 fps cap, up to 2.0× pixel ratio, 1024×1024 caustic map updated at 60 Hz, 132 sunbeams, 110 background fish, and full shark mesh detail across all distances.
    The renderer requests high performance from the browser (`powerPreference: 'high-performance'`), utilising discrete or external GPUs where present.

The scene contains sharks on continuous swimming paths, jellyfish, schooling fish, drifting particles and sunbeams from a wavy surface above the camera. Sharks have modelled eyes, nostrils, mouths, gills and curved fins, a pale belly and fine skin texture. Light patterns move across their backs. The scene is silent and has no time limit.

The moon-jelly-inspired models have curved translucent bells with an inner surface, radial canals, four modelled internal horseshoes, four folded oral arms with oval cross-sections and 56 fine tentacles. Each animal retains a separate drift position and pulse phase. The bell stays mostly upright with a small tilt in the current; the animals do not steer or school like fish. Each bell contracts quickly and recovers more slowly, with a small surge along its swimming direction and trailing tissue moving in the current. Translucent bells catch forward sun backlighting, while bells, internal organs and oral arms sample the wave-projected caustic light map. Overhead sharks cast soft shadows onto jellyfish passing below. At night, bells display subtle pulse-synchronised bioluminescence along their radial canals. Their rhythms differ, and their paths stay bounded without visible respawns. Pause freezes their swimming and the speed slider affects them too; mouse interaction still works while paused. These are procedural visual approximations; transparency does not trace optical refraction through the bell or calculate multi-bounce internal scattering.

Each species shares three subtly asymmetric body shapes. Skin colour, mottling, roughness and healed marks vary between individuals. Larger, irregular scars remain visible at the permitted camera distance; their textured edges soften with distance. Shark bodies, fins, mouth linings and teeth are constructed using indexed buffer geometries with precalculated normals and shared index buffers, eliminating duplicate vertex transformations and significantly reducing GPU vertex processing and memory bandwidth. Distant sharks take advantage of distance level-of-detail (LOD) simplifications. Tail beat frequency and amplitude respond dynamically to swimming speed, forward thrust, acceleration and navigation manoeuvres. Pectoral and dorsal fin tips flex elastically under angular turning rates and user dragging forces. Five coherent gill slits bow toward the tail on each side, ventilating with sequential, phase-offset breathing expansions, with pixel filtering to reduce edge flicker. Several larger sharks are accompanied by symbiotic companion fish (procedural remoras or pilot fish) rendered in a single instanced draw call; they undulate in sympathy with host spine flexion and receive projected caustics and host shadows.

Background scenery features schools of streamlined procedural pelagic fish with forked tails, countershaded silvery flanks, GPU-animated tail beats and individual local steering. Each fish separates from close neighbours, aligns with nearby schoolmates, seeks its own goal and regroups after a threat passes. A fixed simulation step makes movement independent of rendering frequency.

A deep freediving training rig (`freediver.js`) features a surface buoy float, a counterweighted vertical dive line, and an illuminated bottom plate. A Constant Weight (CWT) freediver in a streamlined wetsuit, monofin, crown-mounted headlamp and safety lanyard performs realistic depth training cycles along the line: a surface duck dive, active dolphin-kick descent through positive buoyancy, a relaxed hydrodynamic freefall through negative buoyancy, a bottom turn at the plate, and a steady rhythmic ascent. A safety diver wearing long bi-fins descends from the surface buoy to meet the ascending athlete at one third of the target depth and accompany them through the final metres. Both divers receive wave-projected caustics and night lighting, part suspended particles with realistic fluid wakes, trigger nocturnal bioluminescent plankton trails, and are avoided by passing sharks and fish schools.

Great whites have a broad snout, small eyes and a gently moving lower jaw. Their mouth includes a folded, mottled lining, raised gum pads and three modelled tooth rows per jaw. Front teeth are relatively upright; the two inner replacement rows fold progressively inward. Upper teeth are broader than lower teeth, with restrained serrations and individual wear. Tooth rows, gums and lining move with their own jaw. Three visible rows are a visual simplification, not a complete count of every developing tooth. The anatomical references are [Florida Museum's white shark profile](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/discover-fish/species-profiles/white-shark/) and [Natural History Museum's explanation of tooth replacement](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/what-can-shark-teeth-tell-us.html), checked from the local computer on 2026-10-04. The models remain procedural approximations, not scans of real animals.

Sharks keep a minimum gap of approximately 3 metres from the camera during swimming and dragging, including while paused. This uses three scene units and a conservative bound around the whole animated animal, so heads, fins and tails stay outside the gap. Depending on orientation, the visible skin may remain farther away.

Lighting follows your computer's local clock. The sun is low on the left in the morning, high at midday and low on the right in the evening. At low angles, the light turns orange-red; at night, the ocean becomes dim blue. The visual day cycle uses sunrise at 06:00, a maximum solar elevation of 65 degrees at 12:00 and sunset at 18:00. It is independent of your location and the season. Swimming speed does not affect the clock. Pause also freezes the lighting; resuming updates it to the current local time.

The surface combines ten wave components with different directions, wavelengths, phases and speeds, plus a slowly varying amplitude. The sun's direction sets the general light direction; each shaft samples its own local wave slope, so the shafts are not locked to one common underwater direction. The same wave field controls the surface and light patterns on shark skin.

The sunbeam effect uses 132 soft strips distributed in three-dimensional water, including entry points near the camera. Snell refraction at each moving surface point sets its ray direction. Differences in neighbouring wave slopes narrow or spread the bundle; capped brightness and smooth edges soften focus changes. A ray stays straight after entering homogeneous water, while its entry direction changes as the wave moves. Sunbeams and caustics soften and dim beneath overhead sharks using analytical spine-capsule occlusion evaluated for up to 24 nearby animals. Penumbra width widens naturally with distance beneath the occluding shark, while forward subsurface scattering through thin fin edges and jellyfish bells preserves translucent edge highlights. Strips behind a shark are depth-tested; strips in front add a translucent light veil. Camera-facing strips approximate volume scattering and use bounded capsule occlusion rather than full ray-traced shadow volumes. Sunbeams disappear at night.

The scene starts with 1,700 suspended particles; the Particles slider selects up to 5,000. About a third occupy nearby water at every setting. A gentle common current carries them rightward and slightly deeper into the scene. Passing sharks and jellyfish part suspended particles around their bodies via analytical potential-flow displacement and leave swirling vortex wakes behind their tails and bells. Much weaker local eddies preserve small differences without making particles race in opposing directions; sparse flakes rotate slowly and turn edge-on. Their brightness samples the same wave-focused sunlight map as the sharks. Shapes soften near the camera, shrink with distance and fade before crossing the viewing plane. Particles fade completely at their volume boundaries before recycling, keeping long sessions populated without visible jumps. The wake and flow model uses bounded analytical equations rather than a full Navier–Stokes fluid simulation. It reuses one fixed point buffer as the count changes and freezes cleanly with Pause.

Light patterns on shark skin, companion fish and jellyfish are projected from the moving waves rather than generated from unrelated noise. A grid of rays refracts through the surface and lands on three horizontal receiver planes at depths of 5, 15 and 30 scene units. Light concentration is evaluated through analytical vertex-Jacobian flux conservation rather than discrete unaccumulated point splats. The resulting multi-plane texture uses trilinear mipmapping and is sampled on animal surfaces with a soft, depth-dependent cross filter that models optical penumbra dispersion. This prevents high-frequency pixel sparkle and temporal aliasing (flicker) as waves ripple. Sharks, remoras, jellyfish and particles sample the light map in world coordinates, blending between the depth layers and applying capsule occlusion when an animal swims above. Surface orientation, water depth and the sun's colour affect the result. Small patterns fade in the distance to reduce flicker. Optical transmission through the water column follows a 3D Beer–Lambert formulation, attenuating red spectral wavelengths faster than blue-green wavelengths with vertical depth and viewer distance. The map stays fixed while paused, updates when the selected lighting time changes, and is completely bypassed at night to eliminate redundant GPU shader passes. With zero sharks, particles still use it.

This caustic model covers a finite area, filters waves below the ray grid's resolution and approximates intermediate depths. It uses bounded capsule occlusion for overhead shadows rather than computing full light transport or multi-animal ray tracing.

Reflection uses the unpolarised Fresnel equations for water (refractive index 1.333) and air (1.00029), evaluated using the camera direction and the local wave normal. Snell's law determines refraction and the transition to total internal reflection. Reflection shows the blue underwater environment; transmitted light shows the sky. There is no fixed silver colour on the waves. Reference: [Physically Based Rendering, section 9.3](https://www.pbr-book.org/4ed/Reflection_Models/Specular_Reflection_and_Transmission). The link was checked from the local computer on 2026-09-06.

The sky, reflected underwater environment and sunbeams are simplified visual models. The surface does not trace reflections of individual sharks, and the scene does not fully simulate light scattering in water.

At night, a fixed star field appears through the refracting water surface. A broad blue fill represents combined starlight and illuminates the sharks. Its brightness is intentionally enhanced for readability rather than astronomical accuracy. Mechanical shear from swimming animals or user interaction triggers transient cyan-blue bioluminescent flashes and trailing eddies in suspended plankton, alongside rhythmic canal glow in jellyfish bells. Sunlight and its beams remain off at midnight. Pause cleanly freezes all autonomous swimming, particle wakes and bioluminescent decay.

This is a browser screensaver. It does not install itself as a Windows screensaver or desktop wallpaper. A hidden browser tab stops rendering and automatically continues when it becomes visible again. Rendering is paced by a configurable frame-rate throttle (24, 30, 40 or 60 fps) tied to the Graphics slider, and the WebGL context requests the system's high-performance discrete GPU (`powerPreference: 'high-performance'`) for smooth rendering on dual-GPU laptops and workstations. In fullscreen, the page tries to keep the screen awake if the browser allows it. With reduced motion enabled, the scene starts paused; click Resume to begin.

Jellyfish start at varied positions and drift with the shared current rather than returning to fixed homes. Comb jellies leave beyond the right edge, wait a variable interval, and re-enter upstream; ordinary jellyfish re-enter farther upstream outside the view. Pause freezes this transport.

The scuba pair follows an approximately seven-minute sightseeing circuit through the wreck, truck cab and bed, cliff edge and bottom stones. They slow at inspection points, maintain adjacent buddy lanes, and aim their torches at the corresponding feature. The route follows the scene Speed and Pause controls.

Freedivers now use native skinned 3D meshes with sixteen named skeletal joints each. Shoulders, elbows, spine, neck, hips and knees articulate independently; arms retain their thickness during transitions. At the plate, the right hand brakes on the line while the left reaches toward the plate. The pelvis sinks around the maintained grip, knees soften, and the body extends before ascent. During freefall the left hand comes to the nose and the right relaxes beside the body. Headlamps, mask bubbles and fin disturbance points follow the posed skeleton. This is a hand-authored animation, not measured motion capture or a calibrated biomechanical simulation.
