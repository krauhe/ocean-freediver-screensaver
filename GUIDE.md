# Hammerheads · The Blue Deep

Open **index.html** in Chrome or Edge. No installation, internet connection or server is required. Keep `index.html`, `ocean.css`, `ocean.js`, `jellyfish.js` and `three.min.js` in the same folder.

1. **Fullscreen:** Click Fullscreen, press F or double-click the open water. Press Esc to exit fullscreen.
2. **Pause:** Press Space or click Pause. Repeat to resume the animation.
3. **Speed:** Use the slider between the buttons.
4. **Animal count:** The Animals slider selects a total of 0–40 animals, divided as evenly as possible between the checked species, including jellyfish. With only one species checked, it controls that species alone. Some animals may swim outside the field of view. The scene starts with 30 animals. Small background fish are separate scenery.
5. **Hide controls:** Leave the pointer idle for 5 seconds. Move it or touch the screen to reveal the controls again.
   Text and sliders also fade out when a control retains focus. Keyboard input reveals them again.
6. **Push, pull and turn:** Grab a shark or a jellyfish's bell. Hold the left mouse button to push it deeper into the water, or the right button to pull it closer. Move the mouse to steer sideways at the same time. A grip near the centre mainly moves the animal; a grip farther from the centre also rotates it. Animals follow elastically and retain some movement after release, then gradually recover their swimming attitude. Jellyfish tentacles yield to movement. One finger drags in the screen plane and can also turn the animal. These interactions work while paused, with the rest of the ocean remaining paused.
7. **Lighting time:** Drag the Lighting slider to any minute from 00:00 to 23:59. The time appears beside it in 24-hour HH:mm format, regardless of browser language. Moving the slider switches to Custom time and holds that lighting setting. With the slider focused, arrow keys adjust one minute; Home and End select 00:00 and 23:59. Choose Local clock to follow your computer again. Switching back to Custom time restores the last manual setting. You can adjust lighting while the animation is paused.

8. **Species:** Check Hammerhead, Great white and Jellyfish independently to combine them freely. The Animals slider redistributes its total between the checked species. Uncheck all three to leave just the water, small fish and particles; the slider retains its setting.
9. **Particles:** Select 0–5,000 suspended particles in steps of 100. The default is 1,700; zero hides them. Animal and particle counts can both be changed while paused.

The scene contains sharks on continuous swimming paths, jellyfish, small fish, drifting particles and sunbeams from a wavy surface above the camera. Sharks have modelled eyes, nostrils, mouths, gills and curved fins, a pale belly and fine skin texture. Light patterns move across their backs. The scene is silent and has no time limit.

The moon-jelly-inspired models have curved translucent bells with an inner surface, radial canals, four modelled internal horseshoes, four folded oral arms with oval cross-sections and 56 fine tentacles. Their three-dimensional paths bring some animals toward the camera while others recede. The bell points along the path, exposing the top, underside and sides as the animal turns. Each bell contracts quickly and recovers more slowly, with a small surge along its swimming direction and trailing tissue moving in the current. Their rhythms differ, and their paths stay bounded without visible respawns. Pause freezes their swimming and the speed slider affects them too; mouse interaction still works while paused. Day and night lighting change their appearance. These are procedural visual approximations; transparency does not trace optical refraction through the bell.

Each species shares three subtly asymmetric body shapes. Skin colour, mottling, roughness and healed marks vary between individuals. Larger, irregular scars remain visible at the permitted camera distance; their textured edges soften with distance. Five coherent gill creases bow toward the tail on each side, with slightly shorter rear slits and pixel filtering to reduce flicker.

Great whites have a broad snout, small eyes and a gently moving lower jaw. Their mouth includes a folded, mottled lining, raised gum pads and three modelled tooth rows per jaw. Front teeth are relatively upright; the two inner replacement rows fold progressively inward. Upper teeth are broader than lower teeth, with restrained serrations and individual wear. Tooth rows, gums and lining move with their own jaw. Three visible rows are a visual simplification, not a complete count of every developing tooth. The anatomical references are [Florida Museum's white shark profile](https://www.floridamuseum.ufl.edu/discover-fish/species-profiles/white-shark/) and [Natural History Museum's explanation of tooth replacement](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/what-can-shark-teeth-tell-us.html), checked from the local computer on 2026-10-04. The models remain procedural approximations, not scans of real animals.

Sharks keep a minimum gap of approximately 3 metres from the camera during swimming and dragging, including while paused. This uses three scene units and a conservative bound around the whole animated animal, so heads, fins and tails stay outside the gap. Depending on orientation, the visible skin may remain farther away.

Lighting follows your computer's local clock. The sun is low on the left in the morning, high at midday and low on the right in the evening. At low angles, the light turns orange-red; at night, the ocean becomes dim blue. The visual day cycle uses sunrise at 06:00, a maximum solar elevation of 65 degrees at 12:00 and sunset at 18:00. It is independent of your location and the season. Swimming speed does not affect the clock. Pause also freezes the lighting; resuming updates it to the current local time.

The surface combines ten wave components with different directions, wavelengths, phases and speeds, plus a slowly varying amplitude. The sun's direction sets the general light direction; each shaft samples its own local wave slope, so the shafts are not locked to one common underwater direction. The same wave field controls the surface and light patterns on shark skin.

The sunbeam effect uses 132 soft strips distributed in three-dimensional water, including entry points near the camera. Snell refraction at each moving surface point sets its ray direction. Differences in neighbouring wave slopes narrow or spread the bundle; capped brightness and smooth edges soften focus changes. A ray stays straight after entering homogeneous water, while its entry direction changes as the wave moves. Strips behind a shark are depth-tested; strips in front add a translucent light veil. Camera-facing strips approximate volume scattering and do not calculate shadows cast by sharks through other shafts. Sunbeams disappear at night.

The scene starts with 1,700 suspended particles; the Particles slider selects up to 5,000. About a third occupy nearby water at every setting. A gentle common current carries them rightward and slightly deeper into the scene. Much weaker local eddies preserve small differences without making particles race in opposing directions; sparse flakes rotate slowly and turn edge-on. Their brightness samples the same wave-focused sunlight map as the sharks. Shapes soften near the camera, shrink with distance and fade before crossing the viewing plane. Particles fade completely at their volume boundaries before recycling, keeping long sessions populated without visible jumps. The flow is an animated approximation, not a fluid simulation or a shark-wake model. It reuses one fixed point buffer as the count changes and freezes with Pause.

Light patterns on shark skin are projected from the moving waves rather than generated from unrelated noise. A grid of rays refracts through the surface and lands on three horizontal receiver planes at depths of 5, 15 and 30 scene units. The ratio of the source and receiver areas determines light concentration; overlapping ray paths add together. Sharks and particles sample the same 1024-by-1024 light map in world coordinates, blending between the depth layers. Surface orientation, water depth and the sun's colour affect the result. Small patterns fade in the distance to reduce flicker. The map stays fixed while paused, updates when the selected lighting time changes, and is not rendered at night. With zero sharks, particles still use it.

This caustic model covers a finite area, filters waves below the ray grid's resolution and approximates intermediate depths. It does not cast shadows between sharks or simulate the full angular distribution of underwater light.

Reflection uses the unpolarised Fresnel equations for water (refractive index 1.333) and air (1.00029), evaluated using the camera direction and the local wave normal. Snell's law determines refraction and the transition to total internal reflection. Reflection shows the blue underwater environment; transmitted light shows the sky. There is no fixed silver colour on the waves. Reference: [Physically Based Rendering, section 9.3](https://www.pbr-book.org/4ed/Reflection_Models/Specular_Reflection_and_Transmission). The link was checked from the local computer on 2026-09-06.

The sky, reflected underwater environment and sunbeams are simplified visual models. The surface does not trace reflections of individual sharks, and the scene does not fully simulate light scattering in water.

At night, a fixed star field appears through the refracting water surface. A broad blue fill represents combined starlight and illuminates the sharks. Its brightness is intentionally enhanced for readability rather than astronomical accuracy. Sunlight and its beams remain off at midnight.

This is a browser screensaver. It does not install itself as a Windows screensaver or desktop wallpaper. A hidden browser tab stops rendering and automatically continues when it becomes visible again. In fullscreen, the page tries to keep the screen awake if the browser allows it. With reduced motion enabled, the scene starts paused; click Resume to begin.
